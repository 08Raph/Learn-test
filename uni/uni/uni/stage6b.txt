#LESSON Engineering economics | 14
Every engineering decision is also an economic one: it spends limited money and time to obtain some value. Senior engineers are expected to reason about that explicitly.

### Cost is more than the purchase price
The **total cost of ownership** (TCO) of a system includes:

- **Build or buy:** development time, licences.
- **Run:** infrastructure, support contracts.
- **Operate:** on-call, monitoring, upgrades, security patching.
- **Change:** the cost of every future modification.
- **Exit:** migrating away one day.

Software that is "free" to acquire can be the most expensive to own. Maintenance commonly accounts for well over half of the lifetime cost of software.

### Opportunity cost
The true cost of doing something is **what you give up by not doing the next best thing**. Engineers are scarce. Six months spent building an internal tool is six months not spent on the product. Always ask "compared with what?".

### Sunk cost
Money and time already spent cannot be recovered and should not influence what you do next. "We have invested two years in this" is not a reason to continue. The only relevant question is whether, from today, continuing is the best use of future resources.

### The time value of money
A sum today is worth more than the same sum later, because it can be invested, and because the future is uncertain. To compare costs and benefits at different times, **discount** future amounts:

```text
present value = future value / (1 + r)ⁿ
```

where r is the discount rate per period and n the number of periods. At 10% a year, 1,000 received in three years is worth about 751 today.

**Net present value** (NPV) is the sum of all discounted benefits minus all discounted costs. A positive NPV means the investment creates value. **Return on investment** is the gain divided by the cost. **Payback period** is how long until the investment has paid for itself.

These are estimates built on assumptions. Their value lies in forcing the assumptions into the open.

### Cost of delay
What does it cost, per week, **not** to have this? A feature expected to earn 50,000 a month costs about 12,000 for each week it is late. Cost of delay turns prioritisation from opinion into arithmetic. Dividing cost of delay by the duration of the work gives a ranking that favours valuable, short work first.

### Build, buy or adopt open source
| Consideration | Build | Buy | Open source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fit to your needs | Exact | Approximate | Adaptable |
| Up-front cost | High | Medium | Low |
| Ongoing cost | You maintain it for ever | Subscription | Your operations and upgrades |
| Control | Full | Limited | High |
| Risk | Delivery risk | Vendor lock-in, price rises | Abandonment; support |

**Build** only what differentiates you. For everything else, someone whose whole business it is will do it better and cheaper.

### Vendor lock-in
Dependence on a supplier has a cost: prices can rise and the product can change or disappear. Assess the **switching cost** realistically. Some lock-in is an acceptable price for speed. Total avoidance of lock-in is itself expensive, in abstraction layers and in using only lowest-common-denominator features.

### Cloud cost
In the cloud, architecture decisions are spending decisions. Costs are driven by compute hours, storage, and **data transfer**, which is often overlooked. Practices: tag everything by owner, review spending regularly, commit to baseline usage for discounts, turn off what is idle, and track **unit cost**, such as cost per customer or per transaction.

### Economies and diseconomies of scale
Infrastructure usually gets cheaper per unit as it grows. Software development often gets **more** expensive per unit of output as teams grow, because of coordination. This is one of the central facts of software economics.

### Risk and uncertainty
Estimates are ranges. Present best, likely and worst cases. Prefer investments that can be made in stages, with a decision point after each, over a single large commitment: this is the **real-options** view, and it is why incremental delivery is economically sound, not merely fashionable.

> Key idea: Compare options by total cost over their life, count the opportunity you forgo, ignore what is already spent, and stage investments so that you can stop.

! Choosing one use of scarce resources forgoes another.
#Q A project has consumed two years of effort and is unlikely to succeed. What should guide the decision to continue?

#LESSON Product thinking | 13
Software has no value until someone uses it to do something they care about. Engineers who understand the product make better technical decisions and are trusted with larger ones.

### Outcomes, not outputs
- An **output** is something built: a feature, a service.
- An **outcome** is a change in behaviour that results: customers complete checkout more often.
- **Impact** is the effect on the business: revenue rises.

A team can ship outputs indefinitely with no outcome. Studies of experiments at large software companies have repeatedly found that a majority of features, tested rigorously, fail to improve the metric they were built to improve. Intuition about what users want is frequently wrong.

### Understanding users
- **Talk to them.** Watch them use the product. Ask about what they did, not what they would do.
- **Jobs to be done:** people "hire" a product to make progress in some situation. Understand the job, and the competition becomes clearer. The rival to a spreadsheet may be a paper notebook.
- **Look at the data,** and at support requests.
- Distinguish what people **say** from what they **do**.

### Discovery and delivery
**Discovery** finds out what is worth building; **delivery** builds it well. Both run continuously. The cheapest way to learn is the smallest experiment that could show you are wrong:

- An interview or a sketch.
- A prototype that is clicked through but not built.
- A "fake door": an entry in the menu that measures interest before the feature exists.
- A manual service behind an automated-looking interface.
- A **minimum viable product**: the least that delivers real value and real learning.

### Hypotheses
Frame work as a testable bet:

> "We believe that showing delivery dates on the product page will increase purchases by first-time visitors. We will know we are right if conversion among new visitors rises by 2% in an A/B test."

Then test it, and be willing to remove the feature if it fails.

### Metrics
- A **north-star metric** captures the core value delivered to users.
- **Input metrics** are the levers that drive it.
- **Guardrail metrics** must not get worse: performance, error rates, complaints.

Beware **vanity metrics** that always rise (total sign-ups) and tell you nothing. Prefer rates and cohorts: of the people who joined in March, how many are still active?

Common product measures: acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, referral. **Retention** is usually the most telling: a product people do not return to has a problem that marketing cannot fix.

### Prioritisation
Ideas always exceed capacity. Frameworks help make the reasoning explicit:

- Weigh **reach, impact and confidence** against **effort**.
- The **Kano** model separates basic expectations (their absence infuriates), performance features (more is better) and delighters (unexpected).
- **Cost of delay.**

Say no to most things. Each feature adds permanent cost: maintenance, testing, documentation, and complexity for every user.

### The engineer's contribution
- **Feasibility and cost:** which of two similar features is a day's work and which is a quarter's.
- **Possibility:** what technology makes possible that nobody has thought to ask for.
- **Quality attributes:** speed, reliability and security are product features. Slow pages lose customers.
- **Questioning requirements:** "what problem are we solving?" often reveals a far simpler solution.

### Working with product managers and designers
The best teams decide together: product on what is valuable, design on what is usable, engineering on what is feasible. Involve engineers in discovery from the start, not only at hand-off.

### Technical products
Platforms, APIs and developer tools are products whose users are engineers. The same discipline applies: interview users, measure adoption, write excellent documentation, and treat the first five minutes of use as a design problem.

### Ethics of product decisions
Metrics can be improved by harming users: manipulative design, engineered compulsion, hidden charges. Optimising a number does not excuse the method.

> Key idea: Building the thing is not the goal. Changing what users can do is. Treat every feature as a hypothesis, test it cheaply, and measure outcomes.
#Q What is the difference between an output and an outcome?
- They are the same
* An output is what is built; an outcome is the resulting change in user behaviour
- An outcome is the cost
! Shipping does not guarantee any effect.
#Q Why run the smallest possible experiment before building a feature?
- To avoid writing documentation
* To learn whether the idea is valuable before paying the full cost of building it
- To delay the release
! Most ideas fail; finding out cheaply saves resources.

#LESSON Software licensing and intellectual property | 14
Almost all software is built from other people's software. Using it lawfully requires understanding intellectual property. This lesson gives general orientation, not legal advice; for real decisions, consult a qualified lawyer.

### Kinds of intellectual property
- **Copyright** protects the expression of original work, including source code, automatically from the moment it is written. It does not protect ideas, algorithms or facts.
- **Patents** protect inventions for a limited period in exchange for publishing them. Whether and how software can be patented varies by country.
- **Trade marks** protect names and logos that identify a source.
- **Trade secrets** protect valuable confidential information, as long as it is kept secret.

### Who owns the code you write
Usually, code written in the course of employment belongs to the **employer**. Contractors' work depends on the contract. Read your employment agreement, especially any clause about work done in your own time. Contributing to open source from work may need permission.

### Licences
A **licence** is permission to do something that copyright would otherwise forbid. Code with **no licence** is not free to use: by default, all rights are reserved.

### Proprietary licences
The owner keeps the rights and grants limited use, typically prohibiting copying, modification and redistribution. Terms may restrict the number of users, the purposes, or benchmarking.

### Open-source licences
**Open source** licences grant everyone the right to use, study, modify and redistribute the software. They fall into two broad families.

**Permissive** licences (MIT, BSD, Apache 2.0) allow nearly anything, including use in closed-source products, provided the copyright notice and licence text are preserved. Apache 2.0 also includes an explicit grant of patent rights.

**Copyleft** licences require that derivative works be distributed under the same licence.

- **Strong copyleft** (GPL): if you distribute a program that incorporates GPL code, you must make the whole program's source available under the GPL.
- **Weak copyleft** (LGPL, MPL): the requirement applies to the licensed component itself, so that it can be used by proprietary software under certain conditions.
- **Network copyleft** (AGPL): extends the obligation to software offered as a network service, even if never distributed.

| Licence | Use in a closed-source product | Must share your changes |
|---|---|---|
| MIT / BSD | Yes | No |
| Apache 2.0 | Yes | No |
| LGPL | Yes, under conditions | Changes to the library |
| GPL | Only if the whole is GPL | Yes, when distributed |
| AGPL | Only if the whole is AGPL | Yes, including for network use |

Copyleft obligations are generally triggered by **distribution**, which is why the AGPL was written to cover services.

### Compatibility
Licences can conflict. Code under two incompatible licences cannot be combined into one distributed work. Check before mixing.

### Source-available licences
Some licences publish the source but restrict use, for example forbidding offering the software as a competing service. They are **not** open source by the accepted definition, however similar they look. Read them carefully.

### Compliance in practice
- Keep an **inventory** of every dependency and its licence: a software bill of materials.
- Use automated **licence scanning** in the build.
- Have a **policy**: which licences are approved, which need review, which are forbidden for your situation.
- Fulfil the **obligations**: include notices and licence texts; provide source where required.
- Check transitive dependencies, not only direct ones.

Failure can mean being legally obliged to release your own source, to stop shipping, or to pay damages, and it surfaces at the worst time, such as during an acquisition.

### Choosing a licence for your own code
- Want the widest adoption, including commercial? **MIT** or **Apache 2.0**.
- Want improvements to stay open? **GPL** or **AGPL**.
- A library you want widely used, with changes to it shared? **LGPL** or **MPL**.

### Contributions
Projects may ask contributors to sign a **contributor licence agreement** or to certify the origin of their work, to make sure the project has the rights to what it distributes.

### Copying code
Code from a website, a forum answer or a book has a licence too. Small snippets copied without attribution can create obligations. Follow your organisation's policy.

### AI-generated code
The law concerning the ownership of generated code, and the use of copyrighted material in training, is unsettled and differs between countries. Practical points: generated code may resemble existing licensed code; you remain responsible for what you ship; and follow your organisation's policy on such tools.

### Other content
Data sets, images, fonts and documentation carry their own terms. **Creative Commons** licences are common for content. An API being publicly reachable does not mean its data may be used freely: check the terms of service.

> Key idea: Code without a licence is not yours to use. Know the difference between permissive and copyleft terms, track every dependency's licence automatically, and ask a lawyer when the stakes are real.
#Q You find useful code in a public repository that has no licence file. May you use it in your product?
- Yes, public code is free to use
* No; without a licence all rights are reserved by the author
- Only if you rename the variables
! Publication is not permission.
#Q What does a strong copyleft licence such as the GPL require when you distribute a program that incorporates GPL code?

* That the whole program's source be made available under the same licence
- Nothing
! Derivative works must carry the same freedoms.

#LESSON Law, regulation and professional obligations | 13
Software operates inside a legal framework. A senior engineer need not be a lawyer, but must recognise when a design decision is a legal one, and involve specialists early. Rules differ between jurisdictions; this lesson describes common patterns, not the law of any one place.

### Data protection and privacy
Many jurisdictions regulate **personal data**. The European Union's GDPR is the most influential model, and laws elsewhere share much of its structure:

- A **lawful basis** is required for processing.
- **Purpose limitation** and **data minimisation.**
- Individuals' rights to **access, correct, delete and export** their data.
- **Security** appropriate to the risk.
- **Breach notification** to the regulator, and sometimes to individuals, within a short fixed period.
- Restrictions on **transferring** data across borders.
- Substantial **penalties**.

Engineering consequences: you must know where personal data is, be able to delete it everywhere, record why it is processed, and control where it is stored. Some categories (health, biometric, children's data) carry stricter rules, and some sectors have their own regimes.

### Accessibility
Many countries require that digital services be usable by people with disabilities, particularly in the public sector and increasingly for businesses. The usual technical standard is the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines**. Accessibility is far cheaper to build in than to add later, and it improves the product for everyone.

### Consumer protection and e-commerce
Rules commonly cover clear pricing, the right to cancel, fair contract terms, honest advertising, and consent for marketing messages and tracking. Designs that trick users into purchases or make cancelling deliberately hard may be unlawful.

### Sector regulation
- **Finance:** record keeping, audit trails, resilience, anti-money-laundering controls.
- **Payments:** security standards for handling card data.
- **Health:** protection of medical information; software that diagnoses or treats may be regulated as a medical device.
- **Safety-critical systems** (aviation, automotive, rail, medical): development must follow standards requiring rigorous, documented processes.
- **Children:** stricter rules on data collection and design.

### AI regulation
Law specific to AI is developing quickly. A common approach is **risk-based**: certain uses are prohibited; high-risk uses (employment, credit, education, essential services, law enforcement) carry obligations for risk management, data quality, documentation, transparency and human oversight; and people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system. Know which category your system falls into.

### Contracts
- **Terms of service** and **licences** set out what users may do and limit liability.
- **Service level agreements** promise specific availability or performance, often with financial remedies. Engineers must confirm that the promise is technically achievable **before** it is signed.
- **Data processing agreements** govern handling personal data on another party's behalf.
- **Non-disclosure agreements** restrict what you may reveal.

### Liability
Organisations can be held liable for harm caused by defective software, for negligence, for breaches of contract and for failures of security. Evidence that reasonable care was taken matters: documented processes, testing, reviews, risk assessments. Regulation of software product liability and of the security of connected products is tightening.

### Computer misuse
Accessing or modifying systems without authorisation is a criminal offence almost everywhere, whatever the intent. Security testing requires **explicit written permission**. Scraping, automated access and circumventing technical protections may breach both law and terms of service.

### Export controls and sanctions
Some technology, notably strong cryptography and certain advanced computing, is subject to export rules, and dealing with sanctioned parties is prohibited. These can affect who may download or use your software.

### Records and legal holds
Organisations must retain certain records for set periods and must preserve relevant information when litigation is anticipated. Automatic deletion has to be capable of being suspended.

### Working with lawyers
Involve legal and compliance colleagues **early,** when the design can still change cheaply. Explain what the system does, what data it holds and where. Ask for the requirement behind a restriction: there is often a compliant design that still meets the goal.

### Compliance as engineering
Encode requirements as automated controls and tests. Generate evidence as a by-product of normal work. "Compliance as code" makes audits routine.

### Professional responsibility
Law sets a minimum. Something can be legal and still wrong. Professional codes ask engineers to protect the public even where the law does not yet require it.

> Key idea: Privacy, accessibility, sector rules and contracts are design constraints. Identify them at the start, involve specialists early, and build compliance in as automated controls.
#Q A sales team is about to sign a contract promising 99.99% availability. What should engineering do first?

#LESSON Business models and the economics of software companies | 13
Engineers who understand how their company makes money make better decisions about what to build and how.

### What is unusual about software
- **Near-zero marginal cost:** the first copy is expensive to produce; each further copy costs almost nothing.
- **High fixed costs,** mostly people.
- **Network effects:** some products grow more valuable as more people use them.
- **Switching costs** and **lock-in.**

Together these favour scale, and tend to produce markets with a few large winners.

### Common business models
| Model | How money is made |
|---|---|
| Licence | A one-off or annual fee for the right to use |
| Subscription (SaaS) | A recurring fee for a hosted service |
| Usage-based | Payment in proportion to consumption |
| Freemium | A free tier; payment for more features or capacity |
| Advertising | Free to users; advertisers pay for attention |
| Marketplace | A commission on transactions between other parties |
| Open core | An open-source base with paid additions or hosting |
| Services | Paid consulting, support or customisation |

The model shapes the engineering. Usage-based pricing needs accurate metering. Advertising rewards engagement, with the ethical questions that brings. A marketplace must build trust between strangers.

### Subscription metrics
- **Monthly or annual recurring revenue.**
- **Churn:** the proportion of customers, or revenue, lost in a period.
- **Customer acquisition cost** (CAC): sales and marketing spending per new customer.
- **Lifetime value** (LTV): the total gross profit expected from a customer.
- **Net revenue retention:** revenue from last year's customers this year, including upgrades and cancellations. Above 100% means growth even with no new customers.

A simple approximation:

```text
lifetime value ≈ (monthly revenue per customer × gross margin) / monthly churn rate
```

With 50 a month, a 80% margin and 2% monthly churn, lifetime value is about 2,000. If acquiring a customer costs 2,500, each new customer loses money.

A common rule of thumb looks for LTV at least three times CAC, and CAC recovered within about a year. These are conventions, not laws.

### Unit economics
Does the business make money on each customer or transaction once direct costs are counted? **Gross margin** is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the service, including infrastructure and support. Software businesses expect high gross margins; AI products with heavy inference costs can have much lower ones, which makes efficiency a matter of survival.

Engineering directly controls a large part of the cost of goods sold. Halving infrastructure cost per customer is a real improvement in margin.

### Churn and reliability
Retaining a customer is usually far cheaper than acquiring one. Reliability, performance and quality affect churn. Framed this way, engineering quality has a calculable financial value.

### Growth and funding
- **Bootstrapped** companies grow on their own revenue, slowly and independently.
- **Venture-funded** companies raise money to grow fast, giving up ownership and accepting pressure for very large outcomes.

**Runway** is how long the cash lasts at the current rate of spending. **Burn rate** is that rate. Engineering hiring and infrastructure are usually the largest items.

### Pricing
Price reflects the value to the customer and the alternatives, not the cost to produce. Engineering must make pricing possible: plans, limits, metering, billing, and the ability to change them.

### Starting something
- Start with a **problem** that real people have, and have talked to them.
- Build the **smallest** thing that tests whether they want a solution.
- Learn and adjust. Most first ideas are wrong in important respects.
- Do things that do not scale at first.
- Technology is rarely the reason a start-up fails. Lack of demand is.

### Technical due diligence
When companies are bought or funded, their technology is examined: architecture, code quality, security, scalability, licences of dependencies, key-person risk, and technical debt. A clean licence inventory and good documentation have direct financial value at that moment.

### Equity
Start-ups often pay partly in share options. Understand vesting, the exercise price, dilution and the order in which investors are paid before valuing them. Their value is uncertain and frequently nothing. Take independent advice before making significant financial decisions.

### Why this matters to engineers
You will be asked to choose between speed and quality, to justify investment, and to cut costs. Knowing the economics lets you argue in the terms that decide the outcome, and recognise when a technically elegant plan is commercially pointless.

> Key idea: Know how your company earns money and what each customer costs to serve. Reliability, efficiency and speed of delivery each have a financial value that you can state.

#LESSON The machine-learning system lifecycle | 13
A model in a notebook is not a product. **MLOps** is the engineering discipline of building, deploying and maintaining machine-learning systems reliably.

### Why ML systems are harder than ordinary software
Ordinary software behaves as its code says. An ML system's behaviour is determined by **code, data and model together**, and the data keeps changing.

- **Changing anything changes everything.** Alter one input feature and every learned weight shifts.
- **Silent failure.** A model that has gone wrong still returns confident answers. Nothing crashes.
- **Data dependencies** are harder to see and to manage than code dependencies.
- **Feedback loops:** the model's outputs influence the data it will later be trained on.
- **Non-determinism.**

A well-known paper from Google described the result as "hidden technical debt": in a real ML system, the model code is a small box surrounded by a large amount of infrastructure for data collection, verification, feature extraction, serving and monitoring.

```diagram
   data collection -> validation -> feature engineering -> training -> evaluation
                                                                          |
   monitoring <- serving <- deployment <- model registry <----------------+
        |
        +---> triggers retraining
```

### Data pipelines
- **Ingestion** from source systems.
- **Validation:** check schemas, ranges, missing values and distributions before training. A changed upstream field is a common cause of failure.
- **Labelling:** often the costliest step. Measure the quality and consistency of labels.
- **Versioning:** a model can be reproduced only if the exact data used is recoverable.

### Feature engineering and feature stores
Features must be computed **identically** in training and in serving. A subtle difference between the two code paths, **training-serving skew**, makes the model perform worse in production than in evaluation, with no error to show for it.

A **feature store** provides one definition of each feature, used for both. It serves historical values for training, correct **as of each point in time** so that no future information leaks in, and fresh values at low latency for serving.

### Reproducibility
To reproduce a model you need the code version, the data version, the configuration and hyperparameters, the random seeds and the software environment. **Experiment tracking** records these with the resulting metrics for every run. Without it, nobody can say which settings produced the model that is in production.

### The model registry
A registry stores trained models with their lineage and a status: candidate, staging, production, archived. It is the hand-off between training and deployment, and it makes rollback possible.

### Testing ML systems
Beyond ordinary tests:

- **Data tests:** schema, distribution, freshness.
- **Model tests:** minimum performance on a held-out set; performance on important **slices**; **invariance** tests (changing a name should not change a sentiment prediction); **directional** tests (a larger house should not be predicted cheaper); and a set of examples that must always be handled correctly.
- **Infrastructure tests:** the pipeline runs end to end; the served model gives the same outputs as the trained one.

### Continuous training
Models decay as the world changes. Pipelines retrain on a schedule, or when monitoring detects drift, and a new model is promoted only if it passes automated evaluation against the current one.

### Maturity
Organisations move from manual, notebook-driven work, to automated training pipelines, to full automation of testing, deployment and monitoring. Not every project needs the last stage. Match the investment to how often the model changes and how costly its errors are.

### Start simple
The first version should be the simplest model that could work, even a rule, with the complete pipeline round it. A mediocre model in a sound, monitored system can be improved. An excellent model with no pipeline cannot be shipped.

> Key idea: In an ML system, data is part of the program. Version it, validate it, compute features identically in training and serving, and build the pipeline before perfecting the model.

! The model sees inputs unlike those it learned from.

- The engineer's name
! All of them determine the result.

#LESSON Training infrastructure and evaluation at scale | 13
### Compute
Training large models is among the most expensive computing there is.

- **Accelerators:** GPUs and specialised chips. Memory on the device is usually the binding limit.
- **Distributed training:** **data parallelism** splits each batch across devices and combines the gradients; **model parallelism** splits the model itself when it does not fit on one device.
- **Mixed precision** uses 16-bit arithmetic where it is safe, cutting memory and time.
- **Checkpointing** saves progress regularly, so that a failure hours into a run is not a total loss. It also allows the use of cheaper, interruptible machines.

The input pipeline is often the real bottleneck: an expensive accelerator waiting for data. Measure utilisation.

### Managing cost
- Track the cost of each experiment.
- Start with **small** models and subsets of data to find mistakes cheaply. Most runs fail for dull reasons.
- Stop unpromising runs early.
- Use interruptible capacity with checkpointing.
- Share and schedule accelerators.

### Hyperparameter tuning
Settings such as the learning rate and the architecture are found by search. **Random search** is usually better than a grid. **Bayesian optimisation** uses earlier results to choose the next trial. All of it must use the **validation** set, never the test set.

### Transfer learning and fine-tuning
Training from scratch is rarely necessary. Start from a **pre-trained** model and adapt it.

- **Fine-tuning** continues training on your data.
- **Parameter-efficient** methods (such as low-rank adapters) train a small number of added parameters and leave the original model untouched, at a fraction of the cost.
- **Prompting** and **retrieval** adapt behaviour with no training at all.

Try the cheapest approach first: prompting, then retrieval, then fine-tuning, then training.

### Evaluation as an engineering system
Evaluation is not a number produced once. It is infrastructure.

- **Offline evaluation** on held-out data, on every candidate model.
- **Slices:** performance for each important subgroup, so that an improved average does not hide a group that got worse.
- **Regression sets:** specific cases that earlier models got wrong and that must stay fixed.
- **Robustness:** noisy, unusual and adversarial inputs.
- **Comparison** with the current production model, on identical data.
- **Statistical significance.** Small differences are often noise.

### Evaluating generative models
There is frequently no single correct output.

- **Reference-based metrics** compare with a known good answer. They are crude.
- **Human evaluation** is the standard, and is slow and costly. Use clear rubrics and several raters, and measure their agreement.
- **Model-graded evaluation** uses another model as a judge. It scales, and must itself be validated against human judgement.
- **Task-specific checks:** does the code compile and pass tests? Is the answer supported by the supplied documents? Does the output match the required format?

Build an **evaluation set** from real, representative cases, including the hard and the adversarial. It is the most valuable asset of an applied AI team. Without it, every change is a guess.

### Benchmarks
Public benchmarks let models be compared. They also become targets: results may reflect tuning to the benchmark, and the benchmark's data may have been seen in training (**contamination**). Evaluate on **your** task and data.

### Offline and online
A model that scores better offline may not improve the real outcome. The offline metric is a proxy. Confirm with an **online experiment**, an A/B test on the true business metric, with guardrails.

### Documentation
A **model card** records what a model is for, the data it was trained on, its performance overall and by group, its limitations, and ethical considerations. A **data sheet** does the same for a data set. They make responsible reuse possible and are increasingly expected by regulation.

### Compression for deployment
- **Quantisation** reduces numerical precision, shrinking the model and speeding inference for a small loss of quality.
- **Distillation** trains a small model to imitate a large one.
- **Pruning** removes weights that contribute little.

> Key idea: Evaluation is infrastructure, not an afterthought. Build a representative evaluation set, measure by slice, compare every candidate with production, and confirm online what you measured offline.
#Q When adapting an existing model to a new task, which approach should usually be tried first?
- Training a new model from scratch
* The cheapest that might work: prompting or retrieval, before fine-tuning
- Buying more hardware
! Cost and effort rise steeply along that sequence.
#Q Why evaluate a model on slices of the data as well as overall?
- To make evaluation slower
* An improved average can conceal worse performance for an important subgroup
- Slices are required for training
! Aggregate metrics hide uneven behaviour.

#LESSON Serving, deployment and monitoring | 14
### Serving patterns
- **Batch inference:** score many records on a schedule and store the results. Simple, cheap and suitable when predictions are not needed immediately.
- **Online inference:** predict on request, within a latency budget.
- **Streaming inference:** predict as events arrive.
- **On-device inference:** run the model on the user's phone or browser, for privacy, latency and offline use.

Use batch when you can. Online serving is much more demanding.

### Serving performance
- **Latency** and **throughput** pull against each other. **Dynamic batching** groups concurrent requests to use the accelerator efficiently, at the cost of a short wait.
- **Caching** identical or similar requests.
- **Model optimisation:** quantisation, distillation, compilation for the target hardware.
- **Autoscaling** is harder than for ordinary services: models take a long time to load, and accelerators are scarce. Keep warm capacity.
- **Cascades:** answer easy cases with a small, cheap model and send only the hard ones to a large one.

For generative models, **streaming** output token by token improves perceived speed, and the time to the first token matters most.

### Deploying a model safely
A new model is a risky change. Use the same progressive techniques as for code, with model-specific additions.

- **Shadow deployment:** the new model receives real traffic and its predictions are logged and compared, but not used.
- **Canary:** a small share of traffic, watched closely.
- **A/B test:** measure the effect on the real outcome.
- **Interleaving** and **bandits** for ranking and recommendation.
- **Instant rollback** to the previous model, always available.

Version the model, its preprocessing and its configuration together as one deployable unit.

### What to monitor
Ordinary service health is necessary and not sufficient. A model can be fast, error-free and wrong.

- **Operational:** latency, errors, throughput, resource use, cost.
- **Data quality:** missing values, out-of-range values, schema changes.
- **Input drift:** has the distribution of inputs moved away from the training data?
- **Prediction drift:** has the distribution of outputs changed?
- **Model performance:** accuracy against the truth, when the truth becomes known.
- **Business metrics.**
- **Fairness:** performance by group, over time.

### Drift
- **Data drift** (covariate shift): the inputs change. New kinds of customer; a new device.
- **Concept drift:** the relationship between inputs and the correct output changes. Fraud patterns evolve; what "spam" looks like changes.
- **Label shift:** the frequency of the classes changes.

Detect drift by comparing recent data with a reference window, feature by feature, with a statistical distance. A simple signal is how far the recent mean has moved, in units of the reference standard deviation. Not all drift harms performance, so treat a drift alert as a prompt to investigate.

### Delayed ground truth
The true outcome often arrives late (did the loan default?) or never (was the rejected applicant creditworthy?). In the meantime you rely on proxies: drift measures, and samples sent for human review.

### Feedback loops
A recommender shows items; users click what they are shown; the clicks become training data; the model learns to show the same items. Popular things become more popular, and the model never learns about what it did not show. Counter with deliberate **exploration**, and by recording what was shown and not only what was chosen.

### Responding
Have a runbook. Options: roll back to the previous model, fall back to a simple rule or default, retrain on recent data, or switch the feature off. A **fallback** that needs no model should exist for every critical use.

### Cost
Inference is a recurring cost that grows with use, unlike training. Track cost per prediction and per user, and set budgets and alerts. For large generative models it can dominate the economics of the product.

### Logging and privacy
Logging inputs and outputs is essential for debugging, evaluation and retraining, and it may capture personal data. Apply minimisation, access control, retention limits and consent, exactly as for any other personal data.

> Key idea: A deployed model can fail without any error. Monitor the data and the predictions as well as the service, release models progressively with a rollback, and keep a non-model fallback.
#Q What is a shadow deployment of a model?
- Deploying at night
* Running the new model on real traffic and logging its predictions without using them, to compare with the current model
- Deleting the old model
! It tests on production data with no effect on users.
#Q What is concept drift?
- A change in the serving hardware
* A change in the relationship between the inputs and the correct output
- A bug in the training code
! The model's learned mapping becomes outdated.

#LESSON Engineering applications on large language models | 15
Building on large language models is a new kind of software engineering: the core component is powerful, probabilistic, and specified in natural language.

### Prompts are code
A **prompt** is the model's program. Treat it accordingly: version it, review it, test it.

Practices that help:

- State the task, the audience and the format required, **explicitly**.
- Give relevant **context**; the model knows only what is in its training data and its prompt.
- Provide **examples** of good outputs.
- Ask for **structured output** (such as JSON matching a schema), and validate it.
- Break a complex task into **steps**, each with its own prompt.
- Tell the model what to do when it lacks the information: say so, and do not guess.

Small changes in wording can change behaviour substantially, which is why testing is essential.

### Retrieval-augmented generation
A model's knowledge is frozen at training time and does not include your private data. **Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) supplies the relevant information at the moment of the request.

```diagram
   documents -> split into chunks -> embed -> vector index
                                                  |
   question -> embed -> retrieve nearest chunks --+--> prompt = instructions + chunks + question -> model -> answer with citations
```

Each stage has engineering decisions:

- **Chunking:** too small loses context; too large dilutes relevance.
- **Retrieval:** vector similarity finds meaning; keyword search finds exact terms. **Hybrid** search, followed by a **re-ranking** model, usually does best.
- **Grounding:** instruct the model to answer only from the supplied passages and to cite them.
- **Access control:** retrieval must respect each user's permissions, or the system will leak documents.

Most RAG failures are **retrieval** failures. Evaluate retrieval separately (was the right passage found?) from generation (was it used correctly?).

### Tools and agents
Models can be given **tools**: functions they may call, such as search, a calculator, a database query or an API. The model produces a structured request; your code executes it and returns the result.

An **agent** runs this in a loop: plan, act, observe, repeat. Agents can accomplish multi-step tasks, and errors compound over steps, costs can run away, and actions can do damage.

Controls: limit the tools and their permissions; require human approval for consequential or irreversible actions; cap steps and spending; and log everything.

### Evaluation
The discipline that separates working systems from demonstrations.

- Build an **evaluation set** of real inputs with expected properties of the output.
- Automate checks where possible: format validity, presence of required facts, groundedness in sources, refusal where appropriate.
- Use **human review** with rubrics for quality, and validate any model-graded scoring against it.
- Run the suite on **every change** to a prompt, a model version or the retrieval pipeline. Model providers update models, and behaviour shifts.
- Monitor in production: sample outputs, collect user feedback, track failures.

### Security
LLM applications have new attack surfaces.

- **Prompt injection:** text in the input, or in a retrieved document or web page, that instructs the model to do something else: ignore its instructions, reveal data, call a tool. There is no complete defence at present. The model cannot reliably tell instructions from data.
- **Data leakage** through outputs or through over-broad retrieval.
- **Insecure output handling:** treating model output as trusted and passing it to a shell, a database or a browser.
- **Excessive agency:** giving the model more permissions than the task needs.

Principles: **treat all model output as untrusted input.** Apply least privilege to tools. Separate the data the model reads from the actions it may take. Keep a human in the loop for anything consequential. Never rely on a prompt as a security boundary.

### Reliability
- **Hallucination:** fluent, confident and false. Reduce it with grounding and citations, and with verification steps, and design the product so that a wrong answer is caught and is not catastrophic.
- **Non-determinism:** the same input can give different outputs.
- **Guardrails:** validate inputs and outputs against policy; retry or fall back on failure.
- **Fallbacks:** a provider outage or rate limit should degrade the product, not break it.

### Cost and latency
Cost is proportional to tokens in and out. Controls: choose the smallest model that meets the quality bar; route easy requests to cheaper models; cache; shorten prompts and retrieved context; limit output length; batch where latency allows.

### Choosing a model
Hosted APIs are quick to adopt and improve continuously, with dependence on a provider and data leaving your systems. Self-hosted open models give control and privacy, with operational cost. Decide from requirements for quality, privacy, cost, latency and control, and design so that the model can be swapped.

### Honest product design
Tell users they are interacting with an AI. Show sources. Make it easy to correct or to reach a person. Do not present generated text as verified fact.

> Key idea: An LLM is a probabilistic component whose output must be evaluated, validated and never trusted with authority. Build the evaluation set first, ground answers in retrieved sources, and limit what the model is permitted to do.
#Q In a retrieval-augmented system that gives a wrong answer, what should be checked first?
- The size of the model
* Whether retrieval actually found the relevant passage
- The colour scheme
! Most failures originate in retrieval.
#Q How should the output of a language model be treated by the surrounding system?
- As trusted instructions
* As untrusted input that must be validated before it is used or acted on
- As encrypted data
! Prompt injection and hallucination make trust unsafe.

#LESSON Governance, risk and responsible deployment | 13
Deploying AI in the real world brings obligations beyond making it work.

### An AI risk framework
Established frameworks, such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, organise the work into functions:

- **Govern:** policies, roles and accountability.
- **Map:** understand the context, the intended use and who is affected.
- **Measure:** assess and track the risks.
- **Manage:** prioritise and act on them.

### Classify by risk
Not every model needs the same scrutiny. Consider:

- **Who is affected,** and how seriously, when it is wrong?
- **Is the decision reversible?**
- **Does it affect people's rights, opportunities, safety or finances?**
- **How autonomous** is it? Does a person review the output?

A film recommender and a system that screens job applicants require very different levels of control. Emerging regulation follows the same logic, placing the heaviest duties on high-risk uses.

### Before deployment
- **Document** the intended use and, explicitly, the uses that are out of scope.
- **Impact assessment:** who could be harmed and how.
- **Fairness evaluation** across relevant groups.
- **Robustness and safety testing,** including adversarial testing (**red teaming**).
- **Privacy review:** what personal data is used in training and in operation.
- **Security review.**
- **Human oversight design:** who can review, override and stop the system.
- **Sign-off** by an accountable person.

### Fairness in practice
- Choose the fairness criterion deliberately; several reasonable ones conflict.
- Measure error rates by group, not only overall accuracy.
- Look for **proxies:** features that stand in for a protected attribute.
- Examine the **data**: who is under-represented, and how were the labels produced?
- Technical adjustments exist, and the most effective remedies are usually better data and a reconsidered problem definition.
- Sometimes the right conclusion is that the system should not be built.

### Transparency and explanation
- Tell people when a decision about them involves an automated system.
- Provide a **meaningful explanation** and a way to **contest** it and reach a human.
- Explanation methods for complex models are approximate. Where explanations matter greatly, prefer models that are interpretable by design.
- Publish **model cards.**

### Human oversight that works
Putting a person "in the loop" does not automatically make a system safe. People under time pressure defer to the machine (**automation bias**), and a reviewer who approves hundreds of cases an hour is not reviewing. Effective oversight needs time, information, authority to disagree, and monitoring of how often reviewers do disagree.

### Accountability
Someone specific must be answerable for each system. Maintain an **inventory** of models in use, with owners, purposes, data and risk level. Keep **audit trails**: which model version made which decision on what inputs.

### After deployment
- **Monitor** performance, drift and fairness continuously.
- Provide channels for users and staff to **report problems**.
- **Incident response** for AI failures, with the same rigour as for outages.
- **Periodic re-assessment,** since the context changes.
- **Decommissioning:** a plan to retire the system.

### Third-party models
Using a provider's model does not transfer responsibility. Understand its limitations and terms, what happens to data you send, how updates are announced, and what you would do if it changed or disappeared.

### Environmental cost
Training and running large models uses significant energy and water. Choose the smallest model adequate to the task, and measure.

### Misuse and dual use
Consider how the system could be used to deceive, to harass, or to cause harm, and build safeguards: usage policies, monitoring, rate limits and refusals.

### The engineer's responsibility
You are often the person who best understands what the system can and cannot do. Say so. Refuse to overstate. Raise concerns through proper channels, in writing. Keep the people affected in view: an error rate is a number of people.

### A culture of care
Responsible AI is not a checklist completed at the end. It is a habit of asking, throughout: who could this hurt, how would we know, and what would we do?

> Key idea: Match the controls to the risk. Know who is affected and who is accountable, make human oversight real, monitor after release, and be honest about what the system cannot do.
#Q Which system warrants the strictest controls?
- A model suggesting playlist songs
* A model screening job applicants
- A model predicting server load
! It affects people's opportunities, and errors are serious and hard to detect.
#Q Why does adding a human reviewer not automatically make an automated decision system safe?
- Humans are slower
* Reviewers under pressure tend to accept the system's output without real scrutiny
- Reviewers cannot read
! Oversight needs time, information and authority to disagree.

@solution
function confusion(actual, predicted, positive) {
  const c = { tp: 0, fp: 0, tn: 0, fn: 0 };
  for (let i = 0; i < actual.length; i++) {
    const a = actual[i] === positive, p = predicted[i] === positive;
    if (a && p) c.tp++;
    else if (!a && p) c.fp++;
    else if (a && !p) c.fn++;
    else c.tn++;
  }
  return c;
}

function round3(x) {
  return Math.round(x * 1000) / 1000;
}

function metrics(tp, fp, fn) {
  const precision = tp + fp === 0 ? 0 : tp / (tp + fp);
  const recall = tp + fn === 0 ? 0 : tp / (tp + fn);
  const f1 = precision + recall === 0 ? 0 : 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall);
  return { precision: round3(precision), recall: round3(recall), f1: round3(f1) };
}

function accuracy(actual, predicted) {
  if (actual.length === 0) return 0;
  let right = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < actual.length; i++) {
    if (actual[i] === predicted[i]) right++;
  }
  return round3(right / actual.length);
}

function mean(xs) {
  let total = 0;
  for (const x of xs) { total += x; }
  return total / xs.length;
}

function meanShift(reference, current) {
  const m = mean(reference);
  let v = 0;
  for (const x of reference) { v += (x - m) * (x - m); }
  const sd = Math.sqrt(v / reference.length);
  if (sd === 0) return 0;
  return Math.round(Math.abs(mean(current) - m) / sd * 100) / 100;
}

function canaryDecision(baselineErrorRate, canaryErrorRate, tolerance) {
  return canaryErrorRate <= baselineErrorRate + tolerance ? "promote" : "rollback";
}

function tokenCost(inputTokens, outputTokens, inputPricePerMillion, outputPricePerMillion) {
  const cost = inputTokens / 1000000 * inputPricePerMillion + outputTokens / 1000000 * outputPricePerMillion;
  return Math.round(cost * 10000) / 10000;
}

function sliceAccuracy(rows) {
  const totals = {};
  for (const r of rows) {
    if (!totals[r.group]) totals[r.group] = { n: 0, right: 0 };
    totals[r.group].n++;
    if (r.actual === r.predicted) totals[r.group].right++;
  }
  const out = {};
  for (const g of Object.keys(totals)) {
    out[g] = round3(totals[g].right / totals[g].n);
  }
  return out;
}

#LESSON Planning a capstone | 14
A **capstone** is a substantial, independent piece of work that shows you can do what this programme has taught: define a problem, design a solution, build it to a professional standard, evaluate it honestly and explain your decisions. Nobody will tell you what to build or how. That independence is the point.

Capstone I is a **production-grade full-stack application**. It must be a real, working product, not a demonstration.

### Choosing a problem
Choose something that:

- **solves a real problem** for identifiable users, ideally ones you can talk to;
- is **large enough** to need architecture, and **small enough** to finish;
- **exercises breadth**: a user interface, an API, persistent data, authentication, deployment;
- **interests you**. You will spend over a hundred hours on it.

Avoid clones of tutorials, and projects whose difficulty lies entirely outside your control, such as depending on data you cannot obtain.

### The proposal
Write a proposal before writing code. It should answer:

1. **What** is the product, and **who** is it for?
2. **What problem** does it solve, and how do you know the problem is real?
3. **What will it do?** The must-have features, and explicitly the features that are out of scope.
4. **What are the quality targets?** State them as measurable scenarios: response time, availability, number of users supported, accessibility level.
5. **What is the architecture,** at a high level, and what alternatives did you consider?
6. **What are the risks,** and how will you reduce them?
7. **What is the plan?** Milestones with dates.
8. **How will success be judged?**

A proposal is cheap to change. Code is not.

### Scope
The commonest cause of a failed capstone is too much scope. Protect yourself:

- Classify every feature as **must**, **should**, **could** or **will not**.
- The "must" list alone should make a complete, useful product, and should take about half the available time.
- Decide what you will cut **first** if you fall behind, before you start.

A small product that is finished, tested, secure, deployed and documented shows far more ability than a large one that half works.

### Milestones
| Milestone | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Proposal accepted; repository, CI and deployment pipeline working with a trivial page |
| 2 | Walking skeleton: one feature working end to end through every layer, deployed |
| 3 | All must-have features, with tests |
| 4 | Hardening: security review, performance testing, accessibility, error handling |
| 5 | Documentation, report, demonstration |

Deploy from the first week. Problems with deployment discovered in the last week are fatal.

### Managing the work
- Use an issue tracker and small tasks.
- Commit often, with meaningful messages. The history is evidence of your process.
- Keep a **project journal**: decisions, problems, time spent. You will need it for the report.
- Review your own progress weekly against the plan and adjust the scope, not the quality.

### Risk first
Identify the part you are least sure you can do, and attempt it first, as a small experiment. If the project is going to fail on a technical obstacle, find out in the first fortnight.

### Academic integrity
The work must be your own. You may use libraries, frameworks and tools, including AI coding assistants where permitted, and you must **declare** what you used and how. You are responsible for understanding and being able to explain every part of what you submit. Cite sources. Respect licences.

### What is being assessed
Not the idea, and not the number of features. You are assessed on **engineering quality** and **judgement**: the design and its justification, correctness, testing, security, performance, operability, documentation, the honesty of your evaluation, and your ability to explain and defend your decisions.

> Key idea: Write the proposal first, keep the scope small enough to finish properly, deploy in week one, and attack the riskiest part early.

- The wrong programming language
* Attempting too much scope to finish to a professional standard
- Too much testing
! A smaller, complete product demonstrates more.
#Q When should a capstone first be deployed?
- In the final week
* In the first week, with a trivial page, so that the pipeline is proven early
- After all features are finished

#LESSON The engineering standard expected | 15
This lesson sets out what "production grade" means for the capstone. Use it as a checklist.

### Architecture
- A documented architecture: context and container diagrams, the data model, and the main flows.
- Clear separation of concerns, with dependencies pointing in one direction.
- At least **five architecture decision records**, each with the alternatives considered and the negative consequences accepted.
- The design fits the requirements. Over-engineering is a fault: microservices for a product with ten users would need a very good reason.

### Code quality
- Consistent style enforced by a formatter and a linter.
- Meaningful names, small functions, no dead code.
- Errors handled deliberately; no swallowed exceptions.
- Configuration and secrets outside the code.
- Dependencies pinned, with a lock file.

### Data
- A schema designed with constraints: keys, foreign keys, uniqueness, checks.
- Migrations under version control, applied automatically, reversible.
- Indexes justified by the queries.
- Backups, and a **demonstrated restore**.

### Testing
- **Unit tests** for the logic.
- **Integration tests** against a real database.
- **End-to-end tests** for the critical user journeys.
- Tests for the unhappy paths: invalid input, missing permissions, failures of dependencies.
- All run automatically in CI on every change.
- A statement of what is **not** tested, and why.

Coverage figures are evidence, not a goal. A meaningful suite that covers the important behaviour matters more than a percentage.

### Security
- A **threat model** with trust boundaries.
- Authentication done with an established library or provider; passwords, if stored, hashed with a suitable algorithm.
- **Authorisation enforced on the server for every request and every object.** Include a test proving that one user cannot read or change another's data.
- Input validated; output encoded; queries parameterised.
- HTTPS everywhere; security headers; secure cookie settings; CSRF protection where relevant.
- Rate limiting on authentication and expensive endpoints.
- Dependencies and images scanned; findings triaged.
- No secrets in the repository, verified by a scan of the history.
- A **security analysis** documenting what you checked, what you found and what you fixed.

### Performance
- Stated targets.
- A **load test** with realistic data volumes, reporting throughput and latency percentiles.
- The bottleneck identified, and at least one measured improvement.
- Front-end performance measured: page weight, time to interactive.

### Accessibility and usability
- Semantic markup, keyboard operation, sufficient contrast, labels on form controls, text alternatives.
- Tested with an automated checker **and** by hand with a keyboard and a screen reader.
- Responsive on phone and desktop.
- Helpful error messages and empty states.

### Operations
- Infrastructure defined as code.
- A CI/CD pipeline from commit to production.
- Health checks, structured logs with request IDs, metrics and a dashboard.
- At least one alert, with a runbook.
- A documented and **tested rollback**.
- Graceful handling of a failed dependency.

### Privacy and compliance
- A data inventory: what personal data is held and why.
- Users can export and delete their data.
- A privacy notice in plain language.
- A licence inventory of dependencies, and a licence for your own code.

### Documentation
- A **README** that gets a new developer running in minutes.
- **Deployment documentation** from which someone else could deploy the system.
- **API documentation**, generated from a specification where possible.
- A user guide for the main tasks.
- The ADRs and the architecture overview.

### Proportion and honesty
You will not do everything perfectly. Where you fall short of an item, say so, explain why, and state what you would do next. An honest account of a gap is treated as evidence of judgement. An unacknowledged gap is treated as something you did not notice.

> Key idea: Production grade means the unhappy paths are handled: invalid input, a hostile user, a failed dependency, a bad deployment. Each claim you make needs evidence.

- A test that the home page loads
* A test proving that one user cannot read or modify another user's data
- A test that the logo is displayed
! Object-level authorisation must be demonstrated.

#LESSON Evaluating your own work | 13
An evaluation is an honest, evidence-based answer to the question: **does this system do what I said it would, and how well?**

### Go back to the proposal
You stated requirements and quality targets. Evaluate against **each one**. For every requirement, produce a row:

| Requirement | Target | Evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search responds quickly | p95 under 300 ms at 50 requests/s | Load test, graph 3 | Met: p95 = 210 ms |
| Survives database restart | No data loss; recovers within 60 s | Failure test, log 2 | Partly: recovered in 95 s |
| Usable by keyboard alone | All tasks completable | Manual test record | Met |

A requirement with no evidence is an unsupported claim.

### Kinds of evidence
- **Functional:** the test suite's results; end-to-end tests of user journeys.
- **Performance:** load tests with the method described: the data volume, the hardware, the tool, the number of runs.
- **Reliability:** deliberate failure tests. Kill the database. Fill the disk. Break the network.
- **Security:** the results of scanning, and of your own attempts to break the system, following your threat model.
- **Usability:** observation of real people attempting real tasks.
- **Accessibility:** automated results and manual testing notes.
- **Code quality:** static-analysis output, complexity measures, review notes.

### User testing
Five people attempting realistic tasks, while you watch and say nothing, will reveal most of the serious usability problems. Record what they tried, where they hesitated and where they failed. Do not explain or defend. Then fix the worst problems and say what you changed.

### Measuring properly
- Use realistic volumes of data. A system that is fast with 50 records tells you nothing about 500,000.
- Run measurements several times and report the spread.
- Report percentiles, not only averages.
- Describe the environment, so that the result could be reproduced.
- Keep the scripts and raw results in the repository.

### Negative results
Report what did not meet its target, and what you tried that did not work. "The target was 300 ms; I achieved 450 ms. Profiling shows 70% of the time is in the full-text query. I tried an additional index (no improvement) and caching (improved repeat queries only). With more time I would move search to a dedicated index." That paragraph demonstrates more engineering ability than a claim of success.

### Critical reflection on the design
Evaluate your **decisions,** not only the outcome.

- Which decisions were right, and what is the evidence?
- Which would you change, and why?
- What did you over-engineer? What did you under-engineer?
- What surprised you?
- How would the design need to change for ten times the users, or a hundred times the data?

### Limitations and threats to validity
- What have you **not** tested?
- In what ways was your testing unrepresentative?
- What assumptions does the system make that might not hold?
- What would a determined attacker try that you have not?

### Comparison
Where sensible, compare with an alternative: an existing product, a baseline implementation, or the design you rejected. Comparison gives your numbers meaning.

### Avoiding self-deception
It is natural to want your work to be good. Guard against it: decide the tests **before** running them, ask someone else to try to break the system, and write the limitations section first.

### The standard
An examiner reading your evaluation should be able to tell exactly how good the system is, in what respects, on what evidence, and be confident that you know its weaknesses better than they do.

> Key idea: Evaluate against the targets you set, with reproducible evidence for each, and report shortfalls plainly. Knowing precisely where your system is weak is the mark of a professional.

- A longer description
* Evidence, and a statement of whether the target was met
- A screenshot of the code
! Claims without evidence are unsupported.
#Q A performance target was missed. What should the report say?

* The actual result, the analysis of why, what was tried and what would be done next

#LESSON The report, the demonstration and the defence | 13
The final stage is communicating what you built and defending the decisions you made.

### The technical report
Suggested structure, in about 6,000 to 10,000 words:

1. **Summary.** One page: the problem, the solution, the main results, the main limitations.
2. **Problem and requirements.** Who it is for, the evidence of need, functional requirements, quality targets, and what is out of scope.
3. **Background.** Existing solutions and relevant technology, with references.
4. **Design.** Architecture, data model, key flows, with diagrams. The significant decisions, each with alternatives and reasons.
5. **Implementation.** The interesting and difficult parts. Not a tour of every file.
6. **Testing.** Strategy, what is covered, what is not.
7. **Security analysis.** Threat model, controls, findings and fixes.
8. **Performance evaluation.** Method, results, analysis.
9. **Deployment and operations.** How it is deployed, monitored and recovered.
10. **Evaluation.** Against each requirement, with evidence.
11. **Limitations and future work.**
12. **Reflection.** What you learned; what you would do differently.
13. **References,** and a declaration of tools and assistance used.

Appendices hold the ADRs, the deployment guide, test output and raw measurements.

### Writing it
- Write for a competent engineer who has not seen the project.
- Lead each section with its conclusion.
- Use diagrams for structure and tables for comparisons.
- Refer to evidence precisely: the figure, the test, the commit.
- Be exact. Replace "fast" and "secure" with what you measured and what you checked.
- Edit hard.

### The demonstration
Ten to fifteen minutes.

1. **The problem,** in one minute, through a user.
2. **The product,** live, following a real user journey. Show the most valuable thing first.
3. **The engineering.** This is what distinguishes a capstone from a product pitch:
   - the architecture diagram, briefly;
   - the test suite running;
   - the pipeline deploying a change;
   - a failure injected, and the system coping;
   - the dashboard;
   - an attempted unauthorised access being refused.
4. **Results and limitations,** stated plainly.
5. **What you would do next.**

Rehearse. Have a recording ready in case the live system fails, and if it does fail, explain what happened: handling a failure calmly is itself a demonstration of competence.

### The defence
You will be asked to explain and justify. Typical questions:

- Why did you choose this architecture over the alternatives?
- What happens if this component fails?
- How would this cope with a hundred times the load? What would break first?
- Show me where authorisation is enforced. How do you know it works?
- What is the weakest part of this system?
- What would you do differently if you started again?
- Explain this piece of code.

Preparing:

- Re-read your ADRs.
- For every major decision, know the alternatives, the reason, the cost, and what would change your mind.
- Know your numbers.
- Be able to explain **any** part of the code. If you cannot explain it, you do not own it.

Answering:

- Answer the question that was asked.
- If you do not know, say so, and reason aloud about how you would find out.
- Concede real weaknesses. Defending the indefensible loses credibility for everything else.
- Distinguish what you measured from what you believe.

### The portfolio entry
Afterwards, turn the project into a portfolio piece: a clear README with a screenshot and a diagram, a link to the running system, the report, and a short video. Someone should understand what you built and why it is good within three minutes.

> Key idea: Show the engineering, not only the product. Know every decision, every number and every weakness, and say plainly what you would change.

#LESSON The distributed-systems capstone brief | 20
Capstone II asks you to **design, implement and evaluate a distributed system**: several processes on separate machines or containers cooperating to provide one service, and continuing to do so correctly when some of them fail.

It is harder than Capstone I in a specific way. In a single-machine application, correct code behaves correctly. In a distributed system, correct-looking code fails under orderings of events you did not imagine. The work is as much about **proving behaviour under failure** as about building features.

### What qualifies
The system must have real distributed-systems content. Suitable projects:

- A **replicated key-value store** with a consensus protocol such as Raft.
- A **distributed message queue or log** with partitions, replication and consumer groups.
- A **distributed task scheduler** with leader election and exactly-once effects.
- A **collaborative real-time editor** using CRDTs or operational transformation.
- A **distributed cache** with consistent hashing, replication and rebalancing.
- A **distributed file or object store** with erasure coding.
- A **stream-processing engine** with checkpointing and recovery.

A web application that happens to run on three servers behind a load balancer, sharing one database, does **not** qualify: the hard problem has been delegated to the database.

### What you must decide and state
Before building, write a design document that commits to:

- **The system model.** What failures do you tolerate: crashes, restarts with lost memory, network partitions, message loss, delay, duplication and reordering? What do you **not** tolerate (for example, malicious nodes)?
- **The guarantees.** Exactly which consistency model? Linearizable, sequential, causal, eventual? State it precisely, per operation.
- **The CAP position.** During a partition, which side keeps serving, and what does the other side do?
- **Durability.** When is a write safe, and against how many failures?
- **The limits.** How many failures can it survive? What happens beyond that?

Vague guarantees cannot be tested. "It is consistent" is not a specification.

### Recommended approach
1. **Read first.** The original paper for your protocol, carefully. Most implementation bugs are misreadings of the paper.
2. **Specify.** Write the protocol as a state machine: states, messages, transitions, invariants. If you can, model it and check it with a model checker on a small configuration. It is the cheapest way to find design errors.
3. **Build a deterministic core.** Separate the protocol logic from the network, the clock and the disk, behind interfaces. Then you can run many nodes in one process with a **simulated** network and clock.
4. **Simulate.** With a seeded random generator controlling message delay, loss, reordering and node crashes, run thousands of scenarios and check the invariants after every step. Any failure can be replayed exactly from its seed. This is how the most reliable distributed databases are tested, and it will find bugs that no amount of manual testing will.
5. **Then go real.** Run on separate processes or containers with a real network, and inject real faults.
6. **Check histories.** Record every client operation with its start and end time and result, and check the history against your consistency model. For linearizability, a checker searches for a valid sequential ordering.

### The invariants are the heart
State your **safety** invariants (for example: "no two nodes believe they are leader in the same term", "a committed entry is never lost or changed", "every replica applies the same operations in the same order") and your **liveness** properties ("if a majority is connected, a leader is eventually elected"). Assert the safety invariants continuously in the simulation.

### Failure testing
At minimum, demonstrate behaviour under:

- a node crashing and restarting, including during a write;
- the leader failing;
- a network partition that isolates a minority, and one that isolates the leader;
- a partition healing;
- slow nodes and delayed messages;
- duplicated and reordered messages;
- a node losing its disk.

For each: what the clients saw, whether any acknowledged write was lost, whether any invariant was violated, and how long recovery took.

### Performance
Measure throughput and latency percentiles against the number of nodes, the request mix and the payload size. Measure the cost of your guarantees: how much slower is a linearizable read than a stale one? Measure **during** failure and recovery, not only in steady state. Explain each result from the design.

### Expect to find bugs
You will find violations of your own guarantees. That is success: document each one as a short case study (the scenario, the cause, the fix). A report claiming that no bugs were found suggests that the testing was not adversarial enough.

### Scope
A correct, well-tested implementation of a small protocol is worth far more than a broad system with unverified guarantees. Leave out membership changes, log compaction and the like unless the core is solid, and state clearly what is left out.

> Key idea: In a distributed system, the claim that matters is what remains true when things fail. State your guarantees precisely, then try as hard as you can to break them, and report what happened.
#Q Why does a web application on three servers sharing one database not qualify as a distributed-systems capstone?

* The hard problems of replication and consistency are handled entirely by the database, not by the student's design
- Web applications are not software
! The project must address distributed-systems problems itself.
#Q What is the main benefit of running the system under a seeded, simulated network and clock?

#LESSON The research capstone brief | 20
Capstone III is an **independent research project** in computer science or artificial intelligence. Where the first two capstones asked you to build something to a professional standard, this one asks you to **find something out**: to pose a question whose answer is not already known to you from the literature, to answer it by a sound method, and to report it as a research paper.

It is the piece of work in this programme closest to what a master's dissertation requires. It remains a project completed on this site, and the certificate that follows is **not an accredited academic qualification**.

### What counts as a contribution
At this level, a contribution need not be a breakthrough. It must be **new to the extent that it adds knowledge**, and **justified by evidence**. Realistic forms:

- A **careful empirical study**: measuring how something really behaves, where the literature is thin or contradictory.
- A **replication** of a published result, with an analysis of whether and why it holds.
- A **comparison** of approaches under controlled, fair conditions that existing papers do not provide.
- A **new technique or improvement**, evaluated against proper baselines.
- A **negative result**: a plausible idea shown, rigorously, not to work, with an analysis of why.
- An **analysis** of a system, algorithm or data set that reveals something not previously documented.

### Possible areas
Algorithms and data structures; systems and performance; distributed computing; databases; programming languages and compilers; security and privacy; machine learning and its evaluation; human-computer interaction; software engineering practice. Choose an area in which you have completed the relevant courses.

### The question
Everything depends on the question. It must be:

- **Specific:** one sentence, with the comparison and the conditions explicit.
- **Answerable** with the time, data and computing power you have.
- **Open:** a search of the literature does not already answer it for your conditions.
- **Worth asking:** someone would act differently depending on the answer.

Poor: "How can large language models be improved?"
Better: "Does retrieval with re-ranking reduce unsupported statements, compared with retrieval alone, for question answering over technical documentation, at equal context length?"

State a **hypothesis** that the evidence could refute, and say in advance what result would refute it.

### The method
Use the research methods course. Decide and write down, **before collecting data**:

- what you will build or measure;
- the **baselines**, which must be strong and fairly configured;
- the **data or workloads**, and why they are representative;
- the **metrics**, and why they measure what you claim;
- the **analysis**, including the statistics;
- the **threats to validity** and how you will reduce them.

Writing this down first protects you from adjusting the analysis until it gives the answer you hoped for.

### Feasibility
You have limited time and computing power. Plan a **minimum result** that would be worth reporting even if everything else failed, and do the riskiest step first. Prefer small, clean experiments that you can run many times to one large experiment you can run once. A modest question answered rigorously is a better capstone than an ambitious one answered loosely.

### Conducting it
- Keep a **research log**, dated: what you did, why, and what happened. Record failures.
- Version everything: code, configuration, data-processing scripts.
- Fix and record random seeds. Repeat runs. Report variation.
- **Automate** the path from raw data to every figure and table.
- Inspect the raw data and individual cases, not only summary numbers.
- When a result surprises you, suspect a bug before a discovery.

### Ethics
If the work involves human participants, personal data, security testing, or any potential for harm, follow the principles of research ethics: informed consent, minimisation, authorisation, and consideration of misuse. On a formal degree programme, studies with people require approval from an ethics committee. Working independently, hold yourself to the same standard, and do not undertake work that would need oversight you cannot obtain.

### The paper
Write it in the standard form: title, abstract, introduction with explicit contributions, related work, method, results, discussion with limitations and threats to validity, conclusion, references. Eight to twelve pages. Every claim supported; every figure self-explanatory; every source real and read by you.

### Integrity
- **No fabrication or falsification.** Report what you found.
- **No plagiarism.** Cite ideas and words. Verify that every reference exists.
- **Declare** all tools and assistance, including AI tools, and how you checked their output.
- **Report negative and inconvenient results.**
- Publish the **code and data** so that the work can be reproduced.

### Peer review and revision
Research is improved by criticism. Seek a review of your draft from a capable reader, respond to each point in writing, and revise. Write a review of someone else's work, or of a published paper, to the same standard.

### What is being assessed
The quality of the question, the soundness of the method, the rigour and honesty of the evaluation, the clarity of the writing, and your understanding of the limits of what you have shown. A clear, well-supported negative result is a successful capstone. An overclaimed positive one is not.

> Key idea: Ask a precise question, decide the method before the data, try to refute your own hypothesis, and report exactly what the evidence supports, with everything needed for someone else to check it.

- After seeing which analysis gives the best result
* Before the data is collected

! Deciding in advance prevents fitting the analysis to the desired answer.

#Q What makes a research question "open"?
- It is difficult to understand
* The existing literature does not already answer it for the conditions you are studying
- It has no answer at all
! The literature search establishes this.
#Q Your experiment produces a result far better than any published work. What should you do first?
- Publish immediately
* Suspect an error, such as data leakage or a bug in the evaluation, and check thoroughly
- Delete the baseline
! Surprising results are more often mistakes than discoveries.
#Q Which baseline makes a comparison credible?
- A deliberately weak one
* The strongest relevant alternative, configured and tuned with the same care as your own method
- No baseline
! An unfair baseline invalidates the comparison.
#Q A carefully tested idea turns out not to work. What is the status of this result?
- A failed project
* A valid negative result, worth reporting with an analysis of why
- Something to conceal
! Knowing what does not work is knowledge.
#Q What must be true of every reference in your paper?
- It was suggested by a tool
* It exists, you have read it, and it supports the claim for which it is cited
- It is recent
! Fabricated or unread citations are a breach of integrity.
#Q What does publishing your code and data enable?
- Faster typing
* Other people reproducing and checking your results
- A longer paper
! Reproducibility is central to credible research.

#W Write the "threats to validity" section for a study comparing two sorting-algorithm implementations by measuring their running time on your own computer. Address internal, external, construct and conclusion validity, and say how you would reduce each threat.
+ Internal: confounders such as background load, cache state, warm-up, input order; reduced by controlled, randomised, repeated runs
+ External: one machine, particular input sizes and distributions; reduced by varying inputs and, if possible, hardware
+ Construct: whether wall-clock time on chosen inputs measures general performance; considers other measures
+ Conclusion: variability and sample size; reports uncertainty and uses appropriate statistics
+ Acknowledges threats that remain
